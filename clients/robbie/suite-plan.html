First the thing nobody tells you

You don't buy a Sola suite. You license one — and that's good news.

Sola Salon Studios doesn't sell individual suites to stylists. You sign a rental agreement and pay a flat weekly rate. The suite already comes with the sink, the cabinetry, the mirror, and the core fixtures, and the weekly rate covers utilities, Wi-Fi, and laundry. That's the "chair and sink and stuff" you're picturing — it's included, not purchased.

The only thing you can actually buy from Sola is a franchise: the whole building, thirty-plus suites, and you'd become a landlord instead of a loctician. That's a different life and a different budget.

Here's why the license is the better deal for you. Buying a building means a mortgage, a roof, a parking lot, and a bank that wants to see two years of tax returns before it will talk to you. Licensing a suite means your own locked door, your own name, your own prices, your own hours — for around the cost of a car payment, starting the week you sign. The permanence you want doesn't come from a deed. It comes from a full book and a brand clients follow.

The actual mission

Move into my own Sola suite in Columbus within twelve months, and spend the four years after that turning it into a six-figure loc business — through price, product, teaching, and a second chair — without ever taking on a mortgage.

Phase 0 days 1–90 · get your house in order

Before you tour a single suite

Every one of these takes an evening, costs almost nothing, and makes you look like a business instead of a hobby when you walk into the leasing office.

  1. Form the LLC

    Ohio Secretary of State, $99, online, same week. Protects your personal assets and is what the suite agreement should be signed by.

  2. Get the EIN

    Free from the IRS, takes ten minutes. You need it for the business bank account and to stop giving out your Social Security number.

  3. Open a business checking account

    Separate from personal. This is the single biggest thing standing between you and a lender or a clean tax return.

  4. Get your bookkeeping honest

    Every dollar in, every dollar out, from today forward. Cash tips included. If you ever want a $15K launch loan, this is the file they read.

  5. Move your book onto real software

    GlossGenius, Vagaro, Booksy, or Square — pick one and put every client in it with a phone number and email. That list is the actual asset. Not the chair.

  6. Count your money honestly

    Target $5,300 lean or $12,600 comfortable to open. The table further down breaks out every line.

What to ask on the tour

  • Exact weekly rate for the suite size you want — and what a bigger one costs, in case you want a double later.
  • What's included and what isn't. Utilities, Wi-Fi, laundry are typically covered. Confirm the shampoo bowl and hot water — not every suite is plumbed, and you cannot do locs without a sink.
  • Water pressure and hot water recovery — run the tap. You wash a lot of hair.
  • Ventilation in the suite itself, for steam and product.
  • Deposit, term length, notice to leave, and annual rate increases. Get the escalation number in writing.
  • Is there another loctician in the building? Ask directly. Ask whether there's any exclusivity by specialty.
  • Move-in incentives. Reduced or free first weeks are common — ask, every time.
  • Who holds the Ohio salon license for the suite address, and what they need from you.
  • Parking after dark, door security, and 24/7 access — you will have late appointments.
  • Signage rights and whether you can paint and brand the suite.

Phase 2 the 30 days around move-in · open

Set it up so it runs itself

The suite is empty and yours. What you decide in these thirty days — your prices, your policies, your booking flow — is what you'll live with for years. Get it right once.

Menu & price

Reprice on the way in

Moving is the only moment you can raise prices without explaining yourself. Columbus retwists commonly start at $125; starter locs run $400 partial to $800 full head. Build a menu with a clear ladder — retwist, retwist with interlock, detox, repair, starter locs, consultation — and price the top of your range where you're best.

Policies

Deposits stop no-shows

A no-show on a four-hour starter loc appointment is a $400 hole in your week. Non-refundable deposit at booking, 48-hour cancellation window, late arrival grace of 15 minutes. Write it once, put it in the booking flow, enforce it without apology.

Booking

Take yourself out of the loop

Online booking with deposits, automatic reminders at 72 and 24 hours, a consultation form that asks about scalp condition and loc history before they arrive. Stop booking clients through Instagram DMs.

The room

Build it for photos and for hours

Clients sit in your chair for hours — comfort is retention. And every square foot is a backdrop. One good lighting setup, one clean branded wall, plants, a place for their bag, somewhere to charge a phone.

Phase 3 ongoing · fill the chair

Getting from four clients a week to sixteen

Marketing for a loctician isn't complicated, it's just relentless. Five things, in order of how much money they make you:

  1. Rebook before they leave the chair

    The highest-ROI sentence in your business is "same time in six weeks?" said while you're taking the cape off. A loc client on a six-week cycle is roughly $1,100 a year. Getting rebooking from 40% to 80% is worth more than any ad you'll ever run.

  2. Win back the ones who drifted

    You already have clients who stopped coming for no real reason. A short, personal text brings a chunk of them back for free. Do this the week you open.

  3. Own your Google Business Profile

    "Loctician near me" is how new clients find you in Columbus. Claim it, load it with real photos of your work, post weekly, and ask every happy client for a review by text within an hour of their appointment.

  4. Post transformations, not selfies

    Before and after, start to finish, scalp close-ups, loc care tips. Instagram and TikTok. Consistency beats production value — three posts a week for a year will outperform anything fancier.

  5. Pay your clients to refer

    $25 off their next retwist for every new client they send. Cheaper than any ad, and loc clients talk to each other constantly.

Phase 4 years 2–5 · own it without buying it

Five levers that take one chair to six figures

You chose to stay in the suite rather than buy a building. That's the right call — but it only pays off if you use the money you're not spending on a mortgage to build income that isn't your two hands. There are exactly five levers.

Lever 01 · price

Raise prices every single year

$135 to $175 over four years is about 7% a year. On a full book that alone is roughly $33,000 more a year, with zero extra hours. Announce it once, in writing, six weeks ahead. The clients who leave over $10 were never your clients.

Lever 02 · product

Sell what they're already buying

Every loc client buys oil, shampoo, and gel from someone. Make it you. Retail carries roughly 45% margin and takes no extra chair time. Target $16,000 a year by year five — or private-label it under your own name and take more.

Lever 03 · recurring

Turn retwists into memberships

A prepaid six-week maintenance plan — retwist plus product, billed monthly. It locks the calendar, smooths cash flow, and makes leaving you feel like cancelling a subscription.

Lever 04 · teaching

Teach what you know

The highest-margin income you will ever earn. Loc care workshops for clients, technique classes for other stylists, a digital loc care guide. Nearly pure profit and it runs whether you're in the chair or not. Modelled at $20,000 by year five — and that's conservative.

Lever 05 · a second pair of hands

Take a double suite

When you're turning clients away, move to a double and bring in a second loctician. Get the structure right first — Ohio has specific rules about independent contractors versus employees, and the wrong setup is a tax problem, not a small one.

The point

Four of the five don't need your hands

Price, product, memberships, and teaching all scale without adding hours. That's how one chair becomes a real business — and why you never needed the building.

If you want money to launch strong

You don't need a loan for the lean version. But $12–15K would let you open comfortable, stock retail from day one, and market hard for six months. Columbus has unusually good options for exactly this:

  • ECDI — Economic & Community Development Institute, 1655 Old Leonard Ave, Columbus 43219, (614) 559-0115. A top SBA microlender built for women, minority, and underbanked business owners. They lend where banks won't, and their Women's Business Centers of Ohio do the coaching free.
  • Ohio Micro-Enterprise Loan Program — $10,000 to $45,000 at 0% interest for businesses certified as a Women-Owned or Minority-Owned Business Enterprise. Get the certification first; it's what unlocks this.
  • Columbus Urban League — loan and grant programs specifically for minority- and women-owned Columbus businesses.
  • SBA Microloan — up to $50,000, delivered through intermediaries like ECDI.
  • MBE / WBE certification through the Ohio Department of Development — free to apply, and it's the key to the 0% money above.

The licensing you actually need

Ohio regulates this through the Ohio State Cosmetology and Barber Board (cos.ohio.gov). There are three separate credentials in play and people routinely miss the second and third.

  • Your practicing license. Ohio's Natural Hair Stylist license requires at least 450 hours covering infection control, scalp care, anatomy, styling, and the laws governing practice. It covers twisting, wrapping, weaving, extending, locking, and braiding. There's also a Boutique Service Registration route for braiding specifically.
  • An Independent Contractor license. Under Ohio's rules, an independent working inside a licensed salon needs this in addition to the practicing license. This is the one suite renters forget.
  • A salon license for the suite address. Ohio issues salon licenses by location. Ask Sola directly, in writing, whether they hold it for the building or whether each suite must be licensed separately — then confirm the answer with the Board yourself.
  • Continuing education every two years to renew.
  • Ohio LLC through the Secretary of State, EIN from the IRS, and a vendor's license once you sell retail product — retail means sales tax.
  • General liability and professional liability insurance. Sola will likely require proof; get it regardless.
The decision hiding in here

The Natural Hair Stylist license is non-chemical only. If you ever want to offer loc coloring, lightening, or any chemical service, that requires a full cosmetology license — a much bigger hour requirement. Decide now, because it changes your menu, your ceiling, and possibly your school plans. If coloring locs is part of the vision, start those hours before you sign a suite, not after.
